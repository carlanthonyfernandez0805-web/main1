What Happens After You're Flagged at Border Control?

Flagged at border control? Here's what it means, what happens next, and the steps to take if you're linked to an Interpol notice or arrest warrant.

SN Sharon Nahari, Managing Partner
• Updated 2026
INTERPOL & INTERNATIONAL ARRESTS

Quick answer: Being flagged at border control usually means your name is linked to an Interpol notice, arrest warrant, or watchlist entry. You may face questioning, detention, or — depending on the notice type and extradition treaty in place — provisional arrest.

Why Was I Flagged?

Being flagged at a border usually means your details appear in one of the following systems:

  • An Interpol notice (most commonly a Red Notice or Diffusion) — an international alert circulated by Interpol member countries requesting law enforcement to locate and provisionally arrest a person pending extradition.
  • A national or regional arrest warrant — for example, a European Arrest Warrant (EAW) within the EU.
  • A domestic watchlist entry — placed by a country's own border or immigration authority, sometimes unrelated to an active criminal case.

Many people are flagged without ever having been formally notified beforehand — this is one of the more distressing aspects of Interpol notices in particular, since they are not always disclosed to the subject in advance.

What Happens Immediately at the Border?

Three things typically happen in sequence: secondary screening, where your passport is scanned and you're pulled aside for questioning; verification, where authorities confirm your identity against Interpol's database or the relevant national system; and then one of three outcomes — release, if the alert is outdated or a case of mistaken identity; detention for further questioning, while authorities coordinate with the requesting country; or provisional arrest, if the notice is active and an extradition relationship exists with the requesting state.

What Should You Do If This Happens to You?

A few things matter more than anything else in the moment:

  • Ask why you are being detained and request to know which country issued the alert, if possible.
  • Exercise your right to legal counsel — do not answer substantive questions about the underlying case without a lawyer present.
  • Request consular assistance if you are outside your home country.
  • Avoid signing any documents you do not fully understand, especially anything resembling a waiver of extradition rights.
  • Contact a lawyer with international arrest experience as soon as possible — the first 24–48 hours are often critical.

Can You Check in Advance If You're Flagged?

Yes. Interpol allows individuals to submit a request to its Commission for the Control of Interpol's Files (CCF) to check whether a notice or diffusion exists against them, and to request its removal if it was issued unlawfully or is no longer justified. This process can take months, so it's best pursued proactively rather than at the border itself.

IN PRACTICE

Being flagged doesn't automatically mean arrest — but it does mean you need to act quickly and carefully. The type of notice and the extradition relationship between the countries involved determine what happens next.
